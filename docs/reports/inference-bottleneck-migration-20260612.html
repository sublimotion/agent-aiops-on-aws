Where the Bottleneck Went

How model architecture, GPU hardware, and serving software co-evolved for LLM inference — and why disaggregation emerged but didn't go mainstream · 2026-06-12

An educational survey. Scope is inference serving, not training (whose optima differ — see the sidebar). Numbers are drawn from public literature and independent benchmarks; vendor-stated figures are labeled vendor and independently-audited ones independent.

THESIS

The binding constraint in LLM inference has migrated — not in a straight line "away from compute," but toward whatever is scarce on the current hardware. Through 2023–2026 that has increasingly meant memory bandwidth, KV-cache capacity, and per-kernel launch/scheduling overhead — while low-precision FLOPs became abundant. The twist the literature insists on: algorithms repeatedly re-spend those cheap FLOPs (speculative decoding, FP4, Mamba-2's matmul reformulation) to buy back the scarce bandwidth. So a FLOP-and-capacity-heavy GPU like Blackwell looks over-provisioned for one specific regime — low-to-mid-concurrency decode and prefill-dominated serving of hybrid/sparse models, today, before kernels are fully fused — yet converts its advantage cleanly (~3× audited) on reasoning, large-batch, and long-prefill workloads. The honest claim is narrow and falsifiable, and the rest of this page is the evidence for and against it.

1 · The original sin: two rooflines in one model

Every efficiency story in inference descends from one fact, named cleanly by Pope et al. in 2022: a transformer's two phases live on opposite sides of the roofline.

  • Prefill (processing the prompt) is compute-bound — it does a big matmul over all input tokens at once, reaching high arithmetic intensity (Pope et al. report ~76% MFU). FLOPs are the constraint.
  • Decode (generating tokens one at a time) is memory-bandwidth-bound — each step reads the entire model + KV cache to produce a single token, arithmetic intensity ~1–2 FLOP/byte. Bandwidth and latency are the constraint; the tensor cores sit nearly idle.

This split is the seed of everything below: KV caching, chunked prefill, speculative decoding, and ultimately disaggregation are all responses to the fact that the same silicon is wrong for one of the two phases at any given moment.

Pope et al., Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference, arXiv:2211.05102.

2 · The capacity era: KV cache becomes the constraint

Once you batch requests to keep the GPU busy during decode, the KV cache — the stored attention state for every token of every in-flight request — explodes. PagedAttention / vLLM (2023) showed that the real limit on serving throughput wasn't compute at all; it was memory capacity wasted to fragmentation and over-reservation. Paging the KV cache like virtual memory recovered 2–4× throughput and made "how many requests fit in HBM" the dominant serving question.

This is the first decisive migration: from compute to memory capacity, and it turned inference into a systems problem as much as a kernel problem.

Kwon et al., Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention, arXiv:2309.06180.

PRACTITIONER VERDICTNemotron-3-Super, 2026-06 — judgment, not literature

KV-cache reuse is not free across architectures — on Mamba-2 hybrids it's currently dead. Prefix caching and PagedAttention assume an attention KV you can checkpoint and splice. An SSM's recurrent state can't be sliced at an arbitrary token boundary the same way. We measured it the hard way: with a verbatim ~3,050-token shared header and the flags accepted (--enable-prefix-caching --mamba-cache-mode all), prefix_cache_hits_total = 0 against 4.2M queries — at every TP we tried, including TP=1. The limitation is the hybrid arch, not the KV layout. Don't budget for a prefix-cache speedup on a Mamba/SSM-hybrid until you've confirmed non-zero hits on your exact vLLM build — the shared-prefix discount that makes RAG/multi-turn cheap on a pure-attention model may simply not exist.

3 · The sparsity turn — and where the cost actually went

The 2024–2025 architecture wave attacked the two biggest costs — quadratic attention and dense FFN FLOPs — but, crucially, relocated cost rather than eliminating it. This is the heart of the story, and it's more subtle than "models stopped needing compute."

A note on terminology: "CSA" (Compressed Sparse Attention), seen in DeepSeek-V4-era vendor writeups, is not a standalone citable paper. The verifiable mechanisms are NSA (arXiv:2502.11089) and DSA (DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp). We cite those and treat "CSA" as an informal umbrella term.

The key correction to the naive story. It is tempting to say "algorithms keep fleeing FLOPs." The record says otherwise. Speculative decoding and NSA deliberately add compute; Mamba-2's SSD explicitly routes work back onto matmuls to recover the GPU utilization Mamba-1 lost being memory-bound; MoE moves cost onto the interconnect, not away. The accurate through-line is "spend the abundant resource (cheap low-precision FLOPs) to relieve the scarce one (bandwidth, capacity, latency)." That single principle explains FP4, spec-decode, and SSD at once — and it explains why a GPU vendor would build a FLOP surplus in the first place: it's the currency these algorithms cash in.

4 · The hardware bet — lopsided, but only on specific axes

If algorithms spend cheap FLOPs to buy scarce bandwidth, the natural question is: did GPU scaling actually make FLOPs cheap and bandwidth scarce? The per-generation numbers say yes — but narrowly. Three findings, two of which refute a blanket "everything stalled" claim:

  • SUPPORTED — the FP8/FP4 frontier is lopsided. At the low precisions inference actually runs, required arithmetic intensity climbs to ~560–625 FLOP/byte, while decode delivers ~1–2. So >99% of FP4 FLOPs are unreachable in decode. The cleanest proof is NVIDIA's own B200→B300 step: +1.5× FP4 FLOPs and +1.5× capacity (192→288 GB) with HBM bandwidth frozen at ~8 TB/s. The one axis under pressure is the one that didn't move.
  • REFUTED — bandwidth doesn't "always" stall. A100→H100 was a bandwidth-rich generation (2.04→3.35 TB/s); SemiAnalysis notes that jump exceeded Blackwell's. And H100→H200 was a deliberate bandwidth-led refresh that improved the ratio (295→206). At plain FP16, the FLOP:BW ratio is essentially flat across the whole V100→B300 span (139→156). The lopsidedness is a recent, low-precision phenomenon, not a law.
  • REFUTED — interconnect is a bright spot, not a bottleneck. NVLink went 300→1,800 GB/s and the non-blocking domain jumped 8→72 GPUs (NVL72). This is the silicon that enables wide-EP and disaggregation. We cannot call it a stalled axis and then celebrate the regimes it unlocks.

And the overhead axis hardware can't fix by scaling: kernel-launch latency is ~fixed (~1–2 µs CPU cost, flat across generations). As kernels get faster, this fixed cost dominates — which is exactly why CUDA Graphs exist. It is worked around in software, not scaled away in silicon.

SemiAnalysis Blackwell perf/TCO analysis; NVIDIA CUDA Graphs (developer.nvidia.com/blog/cuda-graphs); GB300 NVL72 (nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/gb300-nvl72).

5 · The bottleneck-migration map

Putting §§1–4 on one timeline. Each innovation is colored by the bottleneck it exposed — the picture is a migration across resource types, with compute (red) being deliberately re-loaded twice.

capacity Mamba (SSM) 2312.00752 — kernel launch MLA + fine-grained MoE 2405.04434 — interconnect Mamba-2 SSD ⟲ 2405.21060 — re-spends compute DeepSeek-V3 + MTP 2412.19437 — interconnect NSA (trainable sparse attn) ⟲ 2502.11089 — irregular access, adds compute DSA (V3.2) gather/scatter compute bandwidth capacity kernel launch interconnect irregular access ⟲ = deliberately re-loads compute

The migration is real but not monotone: it sweeps from compute→capacity→launch→interconnect→irregular-access, with two deliberate returns to compute (Mamba-2 SSD, NSA). "Away from FLOPs" is the wrong summary; "toward the scarce resource, paying in cheap FLOPs" is the right one.

6 · Software's answer — and its ceiling

If the live bottlenecks are launch overhead and bandwidth, the near-term wins are software, and they're large:

  • Chunked prefill (SARATHI / Sarathi-Serve) piggybacks decode steps onto prefill chunks, resolving the prefill/decode tension inside one engine: up to 10× decode throughput and 2.6× (Mistral-7B) to 5.6× (Falcon-180B) serving capacity over vLLM — no KV transfer, no second pool. 2308.16369, 2403.02310
  • CUDA graphs (piecewise → full capture) amortize the fixed ~1–2 µs launch cost; kernel fusion (grouped-expert GEMMs, fused SSM scans) collapses many small launches into few large ones.
  • Megakernels are the frontier: Hazy Research's "No Bubbles" fuses the entire forward pass into one resident kernel — 2.5× (H100) / 3.5× (B200) over vLLM, lifting bandwidth utilization from ~50% to 78%. hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles
But software has a hard ceiling — and the ceiling hands the regime back to hardware. Two caveats keep this honest. (1) The megakernel result is measured at batch 1 on Llama-3.2-1B — a low-latency single-stream regime, not throughput serving; it's a research self-report, not yet third-party reproduced. (2) Even at its optimum it remains "strongly memory bound" at 78% bandwidth. Once software has extracted the available bandwidth, the only remaining lever is more bandwidth — i.e. new silicon (HBM4). Software is a one-time catch-up that erases the launch-bound deficit; it is not a perpetual substitute for hardware. This is the single most important reason the "FLOPs don't matter" framing is transient, not structural.
PRACTITIONER VERDICTNemotron-3-Super on B200, 2026-06 — judgment, not literature

Before you tune a kernel config, confirm which kernel is actually running. Our hybrid-MoE box was launch/scheduling-bound at its SLO knee (DCGM at c192: SM ~50%, HBM ~15%, tensor ~11%, ~701 W) — textbook "software lever, not hardware lever" territory. So we built a tuned fused-MoE tile config and mounted it. Zero delta (c192 p50 6559/p90 10621 vs 6048/9051 — noise). Why: production auto-selected the FLASHINFER_TRTLLM FP8 MoE backend, whose vendor-autotuned GEMMs never read the Triton tile JSON. The "Using default MoE config — sub-optimal" warning we were chasing was for a different (Mamba SSU) kernel. Rule: grep the serving log for the active Fp8 MoE backend before tuning — on modern Blackwell/Hopper vLLM with per-tensor FP8 the default is FlashInfer TRTLLM, not Triton, and the tile tuner is inert.

And the corollary that saved us a GPU upgrade: a launch-bound knee means a bigger chip is a no-op. B300 over B200 adds only HBM capacity (113× over-provisioned here) and FP4 FLOPs (89% idle) — HBM bandwidth is identical at ~8 TB/s, the one axis under any pressure. We stayed on B200.

7 · The payoff: why disaggregation emerged — and why it isn't mainstream

Disaggregation is the bottleneck-migration thesis made physical: if prefill and decode have opposite rooflines, put them on separate GPU pools, each provisioned and batched for its own constraint. The KV cache produced by prefill is shipped to the decode pool over the interconnect.

First, decompose what decode actually reads

The whole argument turns on one decomposition that's easy to get wrong. Per generated token, decode moves:

decode bytes/token = active-weight read (active_params × dtype) + KV read (cache_bytes × dtype)

Attention innovation (GQA, MLA, Mamba, sparse) only shrinks the second term. It does nothing to the first. So which term dominates decides whether a model stays bandwidth-bound — and that, not "is it a frontier model," is what governs the disagg case. Worked from our own measured rooflines:

ModelActive wt read /tokKV read /tokWeight shareDecode is…
Nemotron-3-Super (12B act, Mamba-2 hybrid, FP8)~12 GBtiny (SSM state)—launch-bound (HBM ~15%)
Kimi K2.6 (32B act, MoE + MLA, INT4)16.0 GB0.8 GB95%bandwidth-bound (AI 7.6 ≪ 630)

The Kimi number is the trap. MLA did its job — it crushed the KV term 20×, to ~0.8 GB, which is why 1T-param K2.6 fits enormous batches in HBM. But that leaves the 16 GB active-weight read as 95% of decode bytes. 32B active in FP8 is not "modest because it's sparse" — it is the bottleneck. So MLA shrinking the KV cache does not weaken the bandwidth case for a 32B-active MoE; the weight term keeps decode firmly bandwidth-bound (arithmetic intensity 7.6 vs the machine's 630 at batch 1). Sparse attention helps capacity and transfer-tax, not the decode roofline. measured: B300 roofline, Kimi K2.6, 2026-05-13

The better heuristic: "multi-node-to-serve," not "frontier model"

"Frontier" is a vibe, not a measurement — and our own data contradicts the lazy version of it. We ran Kimi K2.6 (1T params, sounds maximally frontier) single-engine to c=512 at 10,437 tok/s on one 8×B300 node, zero disaggregation. By "frontier → disagg" it should have needed splitting. It didn't. So parameter count is the wrong axis. The screen that actually predicts disagg is:

Does serving the SLO-target concurrency require more than one node?

If the model + the KV for your target concurrency fit and saturate one node, intra-node NVLink hides prefill/decode interference cheaply, decode batches sit past the compute crossover (so there's no expert-parallelism pressure), and single-engine chunked prefill wins. You cross into disagg territory only when a second node is forced — by model size, by 100K+ context whose prefill alone exhausts a node, or by QPS beyond one box. At that point you're already paying cross-node interconnect for the model itself, so the KV-transfer tax is incremental rather than new.

Three measurable gates — disagg wins only if all three fire

The single screen above is the fast version. Underneath it are three independent conditions, each a number you can measure on existing kit rather than a category:

GateWhat it testsHow to measureKimi K2.6 (1 node)
1 · Different parallelismDo prefill & decode want different TP/EP? (the one thing a single engine cannot fake)Decode's BW→compute crossover batch B*. If KV budget holds batches ≫ B* on one node, no EP pressure.FAILS — B*≈92, 1.6 TB KV holds ≫92 seqs → no EP128 need

3 · SaturationIs one node actually full at peak QPS? (else splitting strands GPUs)Single-node GPU util at peak QPS.FAILS — c=512 = 16% of BW ceiling; headroom remains

The governing inequality is a net, not the interconnect tax alone:

disagg wins ⟺ gain(heterogeneous config) > KV/state transfer tax + utilization loss(two pools) + ops complexity(ratio tuning, failure domains)

Three subtleties that the one-liner "useful when phases need different config, if interconnect isn't a penalty" drops — and each can independently kill the case:

  • Load is co-equal with config, not secondary. Disagg splits one fleet into two pools that must each stay saturated, at a fixed prefill:decode ratio matching your ISL:OSL mix. At low or spiky QPS one pool idles while the other bottlenecks — co-location would have kept all GPUs busy. "Different config + cheap interconnect" can both be true and disagg still loses at 5 RPS. This is Gate 3, and it's the biggest single reason disagg is frontier-only.
  • The transfer tax is more than interconnect bandwidth. It's KV + state bytes per request, and layout matters: MLA's latent KV shrinks it ~20× (which is exactly why frontier MoE has a low tax and cross-node disagg is viable), whereas Mamba conv/SSM state needed a dedicated layout fix just to be transferable. It also adds latency to the prefill→decode handoff — a TTFT cost even on a fast link, not just bandwidth pressure. In our prior testing a TCP fallback (no GPUDirect RDMA / NVLink) turned a 355 ms TTFT into 10+ s; EFA-class networking pays a CPU bounce.
  • A simpler substitute clears Gate 2 for most. Chunked prefill (Sarathi, §6) resolves prefill/decode interference inside one engine at 2.6–5.6× — no router, no second failure domain, no KV transfer. Only Gate 1 (genuinely different parallelism) is something one engine cannot do; interference alone (Gate 2) has this cheaper answer.
  • Operational cost is real. Disagg adds a KV router, a transport layer (NIXL), pod gang-coordination, and a new failure mode (prefill pool dies → decode starves). Homogeneous replicas autoscale and recover trivially.

Synthesis — emerged but not mainstream. Disaggregation emerged because prefill and decode genuinely have opposite rooflines, and once a model is forced onto multiple nodes the goodput/cost wins (1.4–7.4×) decisively beat a KV-transfer cost that is now incremental (you're already paying cross-node interconnect for the model itself). It isn't mainstream because the real predictor is "does serving the SLO require more than one node," not parameter count — and most production models, including a 1T-param one like K2.6 in our own benchmark, fit and saturate a single node, where intra-node NVLink hides interference and chunked-prefill aggregation already clears Gate 2 at a fraction of the complexity. Only Gate 1 — prefill and decode genuinely wanting different parallelism — is something a single engine cannot fake, and that only appears once you've spilled across nodes. The two techniques are complementary, not rival: chunked prefill keeps each disaggregated pool compute-saturated (TetriInfer, 2401.11181). Production systems (NVIDIA Dynamo GA, vLLM disagg, llm-d, SGLang PD) are maturing the orchestration; what would move the crossover down is cheap KV transfer (compressed MLA/NSA latents), ubiquitous RDMA, and a lower-QPS break-even.

PRACTITIONER VERDICTour deployments, 2026-06 — judgment, not literature

Weighing the three forces as they actually stand — and all three are moving against disagg:

  • Models shrink the problem: MLA/GQA cut KV, MoE means a "1T-param" model has 32B active and fits one node (we ran K2.6 to c=512 single-engine), hybrid/Mamba is launch- not bandwidth-bound. The models that force multi-node are a handful of frontier deployments, not the standard catalog.
  • Interconnect is the practical killer on rented cloud: disagg needs near-free KV transfer (GPUDirect RDMA / NVLink between pools). On commodity AWS (EFA, CPU bounce) the cross-node handoff adds real TTFT — we watched a TCP fallback turn 355 ms into 10+ s. The moment you're forced cross-node is the moment you lack the fabric that makes disagg pay.
  • SLOs for standard use cases (RAG, chat, copilots: few-K in / few-hundred out, 5–50 RPS) sit where chunked prefill on replicas already meets SLO — Gate 2 cleared with no router, no second failure domain.

Decision rule: single node holds model + target-concurrency KV → replicas + chunked prefill (~95% of deployments; disagg is over-engineering). Forced multi-node and RDMA/NVLink fabric and sustained high QPS → disagg earns its keep (frontier providers, largest internal platforms). In between → still replicas, because the ops tax (gang scheduling, KV router, prefill-dies-decode-starves failure mode, ratio tuning) outweighs the marginal goodput.

Bottom line: realistically practical for maybe a dozen frontier operators; for everyone else a premature optimization on a normal RAG/chat workload. Its near-term home is inside managed serving frameworks — you'll consume it as a flag, not architect it yourself.

A thesis is only useful if it states its own boundary. Here is the strongest evidence against the "FLOPs are over-provisioned" reading — and it's substantial. Note the gap between independently audited numbers and the vendor multipliers they're often quoted as.

So the regimes where FLOP-and-capacity silicon clearly pays off:

  • Reasoning / long-decode (short prompt, thousands of output tokens) — decode-dominated, but large-batch decode is compute-engaging and spec-decode pays off; the direction of travel (test-time compute) inflates this.
  • Large-batch high-throughput serving — big GEMMs saturate tensor cores; this is where FP4 and per-MW TCO win.
  • Long-context prefill — compute-bound by construction; more FLOPs directly cut TTFT.
  • Capacity-bound models — when the weights simply don't fit a smaller GPU, capacity is binding regardless of FLOP utilization.

"Spec-decode pays off" is engine- and config-specific — never assume cross-engine transfer. On the same Nemotron-3-Super FP8 model: on B200/vLLM 0.18.1/TP2, both draft paths were dead — MTP OOM/SIGKilled, n-gram crashed in CUDA-graph capture. On g7e/SGLang/TP8, EAGLE/MTP worked (accept 0.81, length 3.2) — but only with --speculative-moe-runner-backend triton (the draft-worker MoE OOM-kills otherwise). Same algorithm class, opposite outcomes, and the deciding factor was the engine's draft-MoE backend lever, not the GPU. The B200 OOM looks like the same draft-MoE OOM SGLang's flag fixes — but vLLM 0.18.1 has no equivalent knob. Re-measure spec-decode per engine and per checkpoint; a published "accept rate 0.8" on one stack tells you nothing about yours. Also measure acceptance at the temperature the model actually wants (this one wants temp=1.0, which lowers acceptance).

WHAT SURVIVES

Stated as a general law — "FLOP scaling is irrelevant for inference; Blackwell can't convert its premium" — the thesis is contradicted by audited evidence (MLPerf ~3×, ~4× TCO at scale). Stated precisely, it survives and is useful: for low-to-mid-concurrency decode and prefill-dominated serving of hybrid/sparse models, before kernels are fused and graphs fully captured, inference is memory-bandwidth- and launch-bound, and a FLOP/capacity-heavy GPU's headline advantage is muted. The bottleneck has migrated toward bandwidth, capacity, and scheduling — with deliberate compute re-loading (FP4, spec-decode, SSD) to trade abundant FLOPs for scarce bandwidth. Software (fusion, megakernels, graphs) is the near-term lever, but it has a ~bandwidth ceiling that ultimately hands the regime back to hardware (HBM4). And "Moore's law is irrelevant" is too glib: scaling didn't stop, it migrated to precision (FP4, now reaching training — arXiv:2509.25149) and packaging (chiplets, NVL72). The part of the law still alive is the part these models stopped buying.

Sidebar — why training's optima differ (out of scope, for contrast)

This survey is about inference. Training inverts most of the calculus: it is throughput-over-latency (no per-token SLO, so you batch enormously and stay compute-bound), it has no autoregressive decode phase (the memory-bound regime that drives KV caching, chunked prefill, and disaggregation simply doesn't exist), and its scarce resources are aggregate FLOPs, gradient-sync interconnect bandwidth, and HBM capacity for optimizer state + activations. So FLOP-heavy, high-NVLink silicon is a cleaner fit for training than for the inference regimes above — which is part of why a GPU optimized for training-style dense throughput can look over-provisioned when repurposed for low-concurrency hybrid-model serving.
